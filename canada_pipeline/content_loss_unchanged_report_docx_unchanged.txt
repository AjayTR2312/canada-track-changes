

Chapter 7. Saskatchewan Climate Change Law and Policy*
I. Overview
7:1. Generally
 	Saskatchewan occupies a fairly unique position among Canadian provinces in the context of climate change. It has experienced remarkable growth in greenhouse gas ("GHG") emissions over the last decade, fueled by a corresponding growth in natural resource production, especially growth in the petroleum industry. Although most of this energy production has been for export rather than domestic consumption, the effect has been skyrocketing per capita GHG emissions in the province. In response, in 2007, the government of the day unveiled an ambitious program to reduce Saskatchewan's per capita GHG emissions, in the form of the Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007.1 However, with a subsequent change of government, virtually all references to that plan vanished from government policy and program documents, and the new government determined to chart a different course in the form of Bill 95, The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases Act (MRGGA).2 Bill 95 was subsequently revised and reintroduced.3 The new MRGGA received Royal Assent on May 3, 2010, but was not proclaimed until 2018 after substantial amendments were made. Together with The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010,4 and its companion, The Saskatchewan Environmental Code,5 the MRGGA implements Saskatchewan's current climate plan. Known as Prairie Resilience: A Made-in-Saskatchewan Climate Strategy,6 this plan builds on the government's 2016 White Paper on Climate Change where the role of technology and innovation as a means of addressing climate change was featured prominently.7 Furthermore, Saskatchewan has rejected the use of a carbon price as a means to reduce emissions.

B. Climate Change Impacts
7:4. Historical Impacts
 	The Canadian Prairie Provinces have seen a warming trend over the last 100 years, with yearly average temperatures increasing approximately 1.5&deg; Celsius--winter temperatures warming by 3.1&deg;C, and summer temperatures by 1.5&deg;.1 The date of the last spring frost occurs about 10 days earlier on average, and the length of the growing season on the Prairies has increased by some 10 to 15 days when compared to the 1940s and early 1950s. There have been fewer days of extreme cold in the winter, snow cover has decreased and spring run-off begins earlier than in past years.2 Within the last hundred years, the Prairies have experienced two periods with higher than normal temperatures and lower precipitation: the dust bowl years of the 1930s, where rainfall was only 60% of normal, and the even more severe drought of 1988, where one-half the normal precipitation was met with higher than average temperatures during the growing season.3
7:5. Projected Impacts
 	The main effect of climate change in Saskatchewan will likely be on the supply of water. Saskatchewan lacks the moderating effect of large bodies of water. Changes in temperature in Saskatchewan are expected to be greater than in those locations located near large bodies of water. This is expected to result in increased evaporation during the warmer summer months without corresponding increases in precipitation. This could result in increases in the frequency and duration of drought conditions in the province. Where water is available, higher temperatures may result in longer growing seasons, particularly in the northern parts of Saskatchewan. Soil moisture deficits are expected to increase.1
 	The timing of precipitation is expected to change, with more winter precipitation, greater supplies of water from spring run-offs and drier summers. Aquatic habitats are expected to be stressed and various fish species and some waterfowl populations are expected to decline substantially. Warmer winters will result in reduced storage of water as ice and snow over the winter and reduced ability to rely upon winter roads as winter transportation to inaccessible areas. Warmer winters will also allow non-native plants and animals to establish themselves, including insect pests, such as the mountain pine beetle, which would otherwise not survive the cold winters.2
III. Legal Framework
A. Constitutional Framework
7:6. Generally
 	The reader is referred to §§6:1 et seq., "Alberta", and §§3:1 et seq., "Climate Change and the Canadian Constitution", for a discussion of the constitutional framework affecting the Prairie provinces. Like Alberta and Manitoba, Saskatchewan did not receive jurisdiction over its natural resources until the Natural Resources Transfer Agreement of 1930. To date, the Government of Saskatchewan has not aggressively contested the federal government's assertions of shared jurisdiction over climate change regulation.1 Instead, Saskatchewan has advocated against measures that would limit economic growth in the province. Recent discussion of a federal carbon tax, however, has garnered strong opposition from the current government of Saskatchewan.
B. Legislative and Regulatory Framework
7:7. Introduction
 	In response to the federal Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act1 that imposes a carbon tax on jurisdictions that fail to implement a pollution pricing system, Saskatchewan released Prairie Resilience: A Made-in-Saskatchewan Climate Change Strategy. Under Prairie Resilience, The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases Act2 ("MRGGA") was finally proclaimed after being first introduced in 2009. The MRGGA, combined with other environmental regulations, constitute the legislative and regulatory framework for climate change mitigation and adaptation in Saskatchewan.
7:8. Environment-- General Legislation
The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010
 	The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 20101 is Saskatchewan's omnibus environmental protection and management legislation. This Act was proclaimed on June 1, 2015, repealing The Clean Air Act, The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2002, The Litter Control Act, and The State of the Environment Report Act, and making consequential amendments to certain other Acts. Upon proclamation, The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010 introduced the Saskatchewan Environment Code and its supporting regulations.
 	Part III of this Act establishes a prohibition against unauthorized discharges2 and pollution. Section 8 creates a prohibition against discharging or allowing the discharge of a substance into the environment in an amount, concentration or level or at a rate of release that may cause or is causing an adverse effect unless otherwise expressly authorized by legislation or approval therein. Section 9(1) then creates a duty upon any person who creates or permits an unauthorized discharge to report such discharge.
 	This Act also imposes a duty to report the discovery of substances in specific situations. Section 9(2) imposes a duty upon any person who owns or occupies land on which a substance is discovered that may cause or is causing an adverse effect. Section 9(3) imposes a duty to report upon any person who, while conducting work, discovers a substance that may cause or is causing an adverse effect. There is a further duty to take immediate remedial action in the event of such discharge.3
 	The Discharge and Recovery Reporting, c. B.1.1, of the The Saskatchewan Environmental Code, sets out the requirements for reporting discharges and discoveries of substances that may cause or are causing an adverse effect. The Environmental Management and Protection (Saskatchewan Environment Code Adoption) Regulations prescribes the form in which reports to the Minister, the landowner, the person's employer, the person in control of the substance and others who may be directly affected by the discharge must be made.4
 	The Environmental Management and Protection Act 2010 applies to greenhouse gases if unintentionally discharged during storage, including discharge from geological sequestration or when greenhouse gases are transported or used as an input in an industrial process.5
 	The Environmental Management and Protection Act 2010 calls on the minister to keep a record of environmentally impacted sites. The Minister has the power to issue a written direction to a person who owns or occupies an area of land or water that contains a substance that may cause or is causing an adverse effect to provide the Minister with a report. Reports on environmentally impacted sites are to be in the form prescribed by s. 11 of the Act and any requirements set out in the code. The Environmental Management and Protection Act 2010, allows the Minister to require a person who is or may be a "person responsible" (as defined by the Act) to conduct a site assessment if the Minister reasonably believes that a site may be an environmentally impacted site.6 Division 5 of this Act directs the minister to establish and maintain a registry of environmentally impacted sites.7 Section 5 directs the Minister to prepare a report, to be known as the State of Environment Report, every two years. This report must identify and present analyses respecting how the environment is changing and identify emerging concerns for the environment.
 	Although none of the current regulations promulgated under The Environmental Management and Protection Act 2010 are specifically directed at climate change issues, certain sections of the Act and certain regulations promulgated under the Act have incidental effects on total GHG emission in that they restrict certain activities and require licensing of certain proponents and developments. Such regulations include the Halocarbon Control and The Industrial Source (Air Quality) chapters of The Saskatchewan Environmental Code, The Hazardous Substances and Waste Dangerous Goods Regulations, The Scrap Tire Management Regulations, 2017, and The Household and Paper Stewardship Program Regulations each of which apply to certain GHGs.
 	When The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010 was proclaimed the permit-based system outlined in the previous Environmental Management Protection Act, 2002 for managing the environmental impacts was replaced by the Saskatchewan Environmental Code. The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases Act, Bill 95, partially proclaimed in January 2018, provides authority for electricity and reporting regulations. An amended Act was proclaimed in full in December 2018, which provided authority for the output-based emissions management framework, including compliance options such as the technology fund.8
The Clean Air Act
 	The Clean Air Act was repealed by c. E-10.22, The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010 (effective June 1, 2015). Much of The Clean Air Act, as it was then, is contained in Part VII of The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010, entitled "Air Quality" and in Chapters E.1.1 "Halocarbon Control" and E.1.2 "Industrial Source (Air Quality)" of the Saskatchewan Environmental Code.
 	The chapters prohibit the operation or alteration of an industrial source, an incinerator or fuel-burning equipment in a manner that affects the emission of air contaminants, without first obtaining a permit. The "Industrial Source (Air Quality)" chapter does not apply in general to oil and gas activities regulated pursuant to The Oil and Gas Conservation Act and The Pipelines Act, 1998, equipment using commercially available fuel for building heating, recreation or food preparation; fuel-burning equipment for road and railway maintenance, seeding, harvesting fertilizing or controlling pests or weeds, intensive livestock operations; on-road or off-road motor vehicles, rail locomotives, boats or aircrafts, or industrial source facilities no longer in operation. The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010 defines "air contaminant" as any solid, liquid, gas or combination of any of them in the ambient air that may cause or is causing an adverse effect. This leaves open the possibility of a regulation or mechanism under this Act for dealing with GHGs. It also raises the question as to whether GHG emissions may already be considered to be causing an adverse effect.
 	Apart from permitting powers, The Environmental Management and Protection Act, 2010 allows the minister to issue control orders9 requiring any person who contravenes the "Air Quality" Part of the Act or the regulations made for the purposes of this Part of the Act to:
  • refrain from emitting air contaminants;
  • control the emission of air contaminants in accordance with directions issued in the order;
  • comply with procedures to be followed in relation to the emission;
  • install, replace or alter equipment;
  • measure the rate of emission of air contaminants;
  • measure the concentration of air contaminants in the ambient air;
  • provide information, statistics and other data;
  • measure or monitor meteorological conditions;
  • carry out studies for investigations in response to specific air pollution problems;
  • refrain from removing, repairing, servicing, replacing or altering any equipment designed to control halocarbon discharge;
  • remove or discharge a halocarbon from a container;
  • store a halocarbon in a specified manner; and
  • report to the director on any of the above.10
 	Where a person fails to carry out the requirements of a control order, the work may be completed by the Minister and the costs recovered against the party to whom the order was issued.11 Where the Minister is of the opinion that it is in the public's interest to take immediate action to remedy, minimize, mitigate or prevent an adverse effect, or where the minister is unable to identify or locate the person to whom an order should be directed, the Minister may take immediate action to take any measure required by the order.


	










	

7:9. Environment-- Environmental Assessment Legislation
The Environmental Assessment Act1
 	The Environmental Assessment Act outlines requirements for environmental assessment of any "development" that, inter alia, is likely to "cause the emission of any pollutant"2 or "have a significant impact on the environment".3 As a result of recent amendments, project proponents first apply to the Minister for a determination of whether their projects are developments and, thus, within the scope of the Act.4 Approval, and therefore, an assessment, is only required if the project is deemed by the Minister to be a development.
 	Project proponents may obtain screening advice regarding expected impact of the proposed development on the environment and as to its relative significance from the Environmental Assessment Branch of the Ministry of Environment. The Branch reviews basic information about the project, such as the scale of the project and processes involved and the proposed location of the project, to create an Initial Environmental Evaluation conducted through the Saskatchewan Environmental Assessment Review Panel ("SEARP"). SEARP is made up of representatives of various government departments and agencies who are assigned to review proposals on behalf of their respective departments or deputy ministers to determine whether an Environmental Impact Assessment ("EIA") is required. In this process, the Branch may request that the proponent conduct public information meetings in order to identify public concerns.
 	If, after consultation with the SEARP, the Branch determines that an EIA is not likely required, the project may proceed if it is developed in accordance with the proposal and so long as it meets all other legal requirements. If an EIA is required, the Branch will prepare project-specific guidelines to provide the proponent with a detailed description of the information that will be required in addition to the department's general EIA guidelines. Project-specific guidelines are subject to public comment for a period of 30 days after they are issued by the Minister of Environment.
 	The proponent is responsible for conducting the EIA according to the project-specific guidelines and submitting an EIS for the project, the environment, possible alternatives and evaluating potential affects of the development on the environment along with mitigative measures and potential residual impacts. The Branch, in cooperation with SEARP, and sometimes the federal government, coordinates a technical review and provides any preliminary technical review comments in relation to additional studies or additional information required to complete the EIS. When the Branch is satisfied with the content of the EIS, it will complete its review and issue final technical review comments to support final decision-making. Upon completion of the technical review comments, the comments and the EIS are released for public comment for a minimum of 30 days. Following the period of public comment, the Minister issues a decision as to whether or not the development is approved, with or without conditions.
 	Whether climate change impacts are considered in the environmental assessment process will depend upon the nature of the proposed development and its likely environmental effects.5
 	Saskatchewan is a party to the Canada-Saskatchewan Agreement on Environmental Assessment Cooperation,6 which establishes a mechanism for the joint review and evaluation of proposed developments having federal and provincial impacts.
7:10. Environment-- Wildlife Protection Legislation
The Wildlife Habitat Protection Act1
 	The Wildlife Habitat Protection Act is the legislative embodiment of a conservation process initiated by the Government of Saskatchewan in 1984 under what was then The Critical Wildlife Habitat Protection Act. Initially, the legislation protects some 3.4 million acres of uplands or wetlands in their natural state. The Act contains restrictions on the sale of designated Crown lands and on the clearing, breaking or drainage of leased Crown land. Use for grazing and haying by cattle producers continues, as does the ability to undertake oil and gas exploration and drilling, provided that appropriate protective measures are employed.
 	In 2014, however, the provincial government removed a 1.3 million acres of land from these protections by reclassifying them as lands of "moderate" ecological value. These lands became eligible for sale with the proviso that the land would remain subject to ecological protections through the use of conservation easements. An additional 525,000 acres were eligible for sale without restriction.2 Consequently, the positive climate benefits from protecting land from breaking, draining, and clearing are at risk.
The Wildlife Act, 19983
 	This Act vests all property and wildlife in Saskatchewan in the provincial Crown. The Act establishes a scheme for the protection and management of wildlife, the administration of hunting and trapping licences, the imposition of penalties for the commission of offences, and the protection of species at risk. The categories related to species protection in this Act are similar to categories used by the Committee on the Status of Endangered Wildlife in Canada ("COSEWIC"). The Act contains prohibitions against the disturbance, capture, killing and trafficking of protected species. It also provides for the development and implementation of recovery plans in relation to designated species.
7:11. Environment-- Product and Building Standards
Building Codes
 	The 2015 National Building Code of Canada took effect on January 1, 2018 in Saskatchewan as the basis for the province's building standards. The enforcement of provisions aimed at improving energy efficiency standards for houses and small buildings was delayed until 2019. Saskatchewan implements a province-wide adoption of the National Building Code of Canada through regulations with some amendments.1 Updates occur on a five-year cycle. Publication of the 2020 National Building Code of Canada was delayed due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic along with the complexity of the proposed changes.2 However, a significant change includes the creation of energy performance tiers for buildings and houses to set out a model for achieving greater energy efficiency.3 Likewise, large buildings only became subject to the 2017 National Energy Code of Canada for Buildings in 2019.4 This Act is binding upon the Crown and is administered by every Saskatchewan municipality. Further updates occurred in 2020.5
IV. Climate Change Policy Development
7:12. Pre-Kyoto Policy Development
 	Early policy development in Saskatchewan was initially driven by electricity conservation concerns. In 1992 the government established the Saskatchewan Energy Conservation and Development Authority ("SECDA"). SECDA was given the mandate to evaluate and report on electrical generation operations and to promote energy conservation, alternative energy and related business opportunities.1
 	In the build-up to Canada's ratification of the Kyoto Protocol, climate change programs were also initiated by the two large Crown-owned energy utilities, SaskPower, the supplier of electricity in Saskatchewan, and SaskEnergy, the Crown natural gas utility. These Crown corporations have often been used as instruments of provincial government policy.
 	In 1995, SaskEnergy, with its transmission subsidiary, Trans Gas Ltd., issued an action plan under the Voluntary Climate Change Challenge Program.2 The SaskEnergy/Trans Gas voluntary actions were directed toward cost-effective measures to reduce transmission losses, especially at compressor stations, and energy conservation initiatives with consumers. SaskPower has also been a participant in the Voluntary Change Registry since 1995; however, the program no longer seems to exist.
 	By 1996, SECDA was disbanded as part of government cost-reduction efforts and most of its responsibilities and remaining programs were transferred to the Saskatchewan Research Council.3
7:13. Policy Development in Early Kyoto Era
 	In 1998, the Government of Saskatchewan established the Saskatchewan Stakeholder Advisory Committee on Climate Change. The Advisory Committee was established to permit government, agriculture, environmental, industry and community stakeholders to exchange information related to climate change matters.
 	In May of 2001, the Government of Saskatchewan announced the development of a Green Print for Energy Conservation to provide residents of Saskatchewan with a long-term provincial energy conservation strategy.1 The objective of the Green Print was to provide a plan for dealing with escalating energy costs by assisting consumers in managing energy costs in the short-term while establishing a longer-term strategy on energy conservation for Saskatchewan. Although the focus of the Green Print was on the reduction of energy costs, it was also intended to deal with Saskatchewan's responsibilities and commitments on climate change.
 	In response to and in anticipation of federal climate change initiatives, Saskatchewan put forward its own perspective on climate change policy in 2002.2 That policy recognized anthropogenic climate change and the likelihood of climate change impacts for the Province of Saskatchewan. The Policy emphasized Saskatchewan's ongoing efforts to implement actions to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, which, at the time, included the following:
  • the establishment of the Saskatchewan Stakeholder Advisory Committee on Climate Change;
  • the enactment of legislation to mandate the use of ethanol in gasoline;
  • the establishment of the Office of Energy Conservation in 2002;
  • the initiation of the IEA Weyburn Carbon Dioxide Monitoring and Storage Project;
  • the establishment of the International Test Centre for Carbon Dioxide Capture at the University of Regina;
  • support for the establishment of a Prairie Adaptation Network and the Prairie Adaptation Research Collaborative (PARC) which funds adaptation research across the Canadian Prairies;
  • participation in the Gull Lake and Cypress Wind Power Development;
  • the establishment of a forest carbon reserve with SaskPower;
  • various initiatives with the Saskatchewan Research Council and other groups.
Saskatchewan's policy was focused on five major areas:
  • public education;
  • carbon capture technology;
  • the development of adaptation strategies;
  • the development of biological carbon sinks in agricultural soils and forests; and
  • energy conservation and alternative energy projects.
7:14. Post-Kyoto Policy Development
 	In 2002, the Government of Saskatchewan created the Office of Energy Conservation. The Office, which was funded by SaskPower, SaskEnergy and Saskatchewan Industry and Resources, was given the purpose of undertaking energy conservation initiatives to reduce energy costs for Saskatchewan residents, businesses and farmers. Again, the emphasis was on energy conservation with a secondary goal of GHG reductions.
7:15. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- A First Report on Making Saskatchewan a Canadian Leader in Energy Conservation and Renewable Energy Development (2006)1
 	In May 2006, then Premier Lorne Calvert appointed Saskatchewan MLA Peter Prebble as Legislative Secretary for Renewable Energy Development and Conservation and tasked him with the preparation of a report dealing with Saskatchewan's options for the development of renewable energy sources and energy conservation programs. This built upon the vision of the government of the day that by 2020, one third of Saskatchewan's energy needs would be met by renewable energy sources. The product of this investigation was Renewable Energy Development and Conservation in Saskatchewan: A First Report on Making Saskatchewan a Canadian Leader in Energy Conservation and Renewable Energy Development. The purpose of the report was to provide direction to the government in achieving those ends. The report contained recommendations which would build upon Saskatchewan's existing wind power, ethanol production, CO2 sequestration and energy conservation programs. Its focus was primarily on the electricity sector and the buildings sector.
 	In the electrical sector, the priority recommendations were focused on renewable energy production and energy conservation through demand management. The report proposed that Saskatchewan's Renewable Energy and Conservation Portfolio Standard would require that a minimum of 50% of Saskatchewan's electricity would be derived from renewable energy sources and electricity conservation by 2025.2 It was proposed that major wind power developments would contribute 500 MW of electricity to the Saskatchewan system by 2015. Some additional savings would be achieved by encouraging net metering for small-scale producers of "green, low impact" energy.
 	Facing forecasted electricity consumption growth and a need to retrofit or replace existing generating facilities, considerable emphasis in the report is placed on demand management.3 Measures were proposed to forecast the consumption by 2025 in order to manage the supply gap.
 	The second policy priority proposed in the report was financial assistance to promote energy conservation and the development of renewable energy sources. Emphasis was placed on programs to create incentives for energy efficiency in businesses and among consumers.
 	The third priority was the conservation of electrical energy and incentives for the use of renewable energy in the commercial and institutional building sector. Proposals included the implementation of an energy efficiency building code standard for new commercial buildings, the promotion of the construction of net zero energy buildings, with emphasis on commercial and institutional buildings and promotion of the adoption of solar energy, with a call for the introduction of "solar rights" legislation.
 	The final priority policies were directed toward the promotion of conservation and renewable energy development. Under this policy-oriented priority, the report called for the expansion of the role of the Office of Energy Conservation and the establishment of an Energy Conservation and Renewable Energy Fund to provide stable funding for conservation and renewable energy initiatives.4
7:16. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- The Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007
 	In 2007, Saskatchewan's NDP government introduced an aggressive plan to reduce per capita GHG emissions by 32%, based on 2004 GHG emission levels, by 2020. The plan called for a further reduction in the following years, so as to achieve an 80% per capita reduction from 2004 levels by 2050. This reduction translated to a 22 tonne per capita reduction in GHG emissions by 2020 (i.e., a target reduction from 71.9 tonnes of GHG per person recorded in 2004 to 49.9 tonnes of GHG per person in 2020)1 and a per capita reduction of 55 tonnes by 2050 (i.e., a target of 16.9 tonnes of GHG per person in 2050). This was expressed by the government of the day as "the most ambitious target in Canada"2 and as being significantly greater than the percentage reduction required for Canada to meet the reduction required under the Kyoto Protocol.3 The plan was promoted as particularly ambitious because of Saskatchewan's cold climate, its energy-intensive economy and its rapid economic growth. Ultimately, Saskatchewan abandoned the Plan, and this goal was not met; Saskatchewan's per capita emissions for 2020 were recorded at 55.8 tonnes.
 	Borrowing language from former U.S. Vice-President Al Gore, the Plan described Saskatchewan as representing a "tipping point" in that, despite its small population, Saskatchewan's efforts to curb GHG emissions would make a genuine difference on the world stage. The Plan recognized the challenges faced by a province having only 3% of Canada's population but which contributed, as of 2004, 9% of Canada's GHG emissions. It also recognized the continuation of the province's rapid economic growth was largely dependent on the continued growth of the natural resource sector, especially fossil fuel based energy.
 	The Plan was based on five "wedges" or areas of emission reduction and climate change policy: conservation and energy efficiency; carbon capture and storage in the oil and gas and electricity sectors; increased use of renewable energy; promotion of the creation of natural carbon sinks; and the reduction of methane and other emissions from the oil and gas and agricultural sectors. The Plan placed significant reliance upon the development of as yet unproven technology in such areas as carbon capture and sequestration in order to meet its ambitious targets, especially for the post-2020 period. As such, considerable emphasis was given to innovation in the energy sector, the promotion of efficiency and conservation, the creation of alternatives to fossil fuels and measures to remove carbon dioxide from the environment.
7:17. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- The Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007-- Conservation and Efficiency
 	The Plan included a commitment to save at least 300 MW of SaskPower's electricity generating capacity through demand side management practices, thereby eliminating the equivalent of one coal-fired generating plant.1 Whether this was intended to mean the closure of a coal-fired plant, or whether the purpose was to simply free SaskPower to export more electricity was not answered by the Plan. Part of the energy savings were intended to be realized through existing programs that encouraged individuals and families to save electricity through better household management, better insulation and more efficient heating and a retail sales tax exemption for energy-efficient appliances. Other savings would be realized from existing programs that assisted communities, commercial and industrial facilities, farms and municipalities.
 	The Plan identified a series of other continuing initiatives. SaskPower would begin a transition to smart meters. Government would promote energy-efficient products and practices by setting new standards, codes and regulations. New technologies would be promoted, such as home energy monitors, in order to make consumers better aware of their energy consumption and to better plan for energy consumption reductions. The Plan reaffirmed a commitment to reduce energy consumption from government's core buildings by 20%, to be realized through the replacement of building components.
 	The Plan also called for incremental measures in the area of conservation and efficiency. Proposed immediate action included the implementation of a government vehicle purchasing policy requiring all vehicles to be hybrid-electric, alternative or flex fuel, or within the top 20% of efficiency in their class. As an alternative to constructing new electricity generating facilities, SaskPower, a traditional vehicle of government policy, would introduce programs to reduce its electrical load by 300 MW by 2017. Eligibility criteria for the Investment Tax Credit for Manufacturing and Processing would be expanded to include more types of renewable energy and energy conservation equipment used to generate electricity. A voluntary, province-wide vehicle retirement program was proposed to support the existing federal program to retire older vehicles. Finally, energy conservation and efficiency initiatives in the agricultural sector were to be supported.
 	On the two-year planning horizon, the Plan called for the establishment of a Climate Change Secretariat with increased funding and responsibility for the coordination of climate change policy and the delivery of energy conservation programs. It also called for the implementation of new energy efficiency building standards, in consultation with industry.
 	On a five-year planning horizon, the Plan targeted continued funding of programs for homeowners that would help them reduce energy consumption, maximize the development of small-scale, low environmental impact local electricity generation in northern Saskatchewan and consult with residents of the North as to their long-term requirements for power supply and transmission infrastructure.
7:18. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- The Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007-- Carbon Capture and Storage in the Oil and Gas and Electricity Sectors1
 	The Plan built upon Saskatchewan's leading role in the study of carbon dioxide sequestration in geological formations in the province, in particular the Weyburn oilfield. When injected in deep, oil-bearing formations, carbon dioxide can be used for purposes of enhanced oil recovery. This gives rise to the somewhat ironic result of greater production of fossil fuels for export. Saskatchewan also intended to rely upon storage and other geological formations containing salt water. Saline aquifers underlie much of the southern half of the province. This was seen as affording an opportunity for low-cost, highly reliable storage of carbon dioxide emissions captured from coal-fired power plants, heavy oil upgraders and fertilizer plants.
 	In order to develop the technology necessary to embark upon this extensive implementation of carbon dioxide capture and storage, Saskatchewan proposed to continue to support its Petroleum Technology Research Center and the International Test Center for Carbon Dioxide Capture, also located in the province. In 2014, the province completed the construction of a carbon capture facility: the Boundary Dam Integrated Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) Demonstration Project.
7:19. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- The Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007-- Increase the Use of Renewable Energy1
 	At the time the Plan was released, Saskatchewan had the largest per capita wind power generation capacity of any region in Canada. Saskatchewan also continues to produce a large amount of hydroelectric power, although not enough to meet its entire domestic needs. Saskatchewan has approximately 889 MW of hydroelectric capacity.1.50 The Plan supported the addition of small-scale, environmentally friendly, electric power generation from independent power producers.
 	In 2005, Saskatchewan became the first province to mandate the use of ethanol blends in gasoline. In 2020-2021, Saskatchewan was home to four commercial-level ethanol plants. In 2020, Canada had eight operational bio-diesel plants.2 As of 2015, one bio-diesel plant was in operation in Saskatchewan.3 A new bio-diesel plant is expected to be constructed in Saskatchewan by 2027.4 The ethanol plants produce grain-based ethanol for blending with conventional gasoline fuel. Saskatchewan has supported the investigation of alternative ethanol production technologies, including cellulose-based ethanol. It has also funded research for the promotion of other alternative fuels, including hydrogen. The Plan called for the establishment of a Bio-Products Centre for Excellence and cooperation with industry to increase sales of ethanol-blended gasoline by 2010. It hoped for the development of a 1.4 billion litre biofuels industry, with resultant job creation benefits, the development of hydrogen production and utilization pilot projects (including fuel station infrastructure) and the implementation of the government vehicle purchasing policy discussed above.
7:20. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- The Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007-- Promoting the Creation of Natural Carbon Sinks1
 	The Plan assumed a national emissions trading system in Canada that would facilitate the promotion of natural carbon sinks. Saskatchewan would achieve global GHG reductions by encouraging farmers to increase agricultural soil sinks (targeting the removal of 25 Mt of CO2 per year by 2012, increasing to more than 37 MT by 2050). Additional CO2 emission reductions would be achieved by the reforestation of 20,000 hectares of land by 2017 (estimated to result in another 4.9 Mt in CO2 emission reductions).
7:21. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- The Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007-- Reduction of Methane and Other Emissions from Oil, Gas and Agricultural Sectors1
 	In agriculture, the Plan targeted emissions from livestock production and the inefficient use of nitrogen fertilizer. Its goal was to develop more efficient management practices and technology that would result in a 20% reduction in agricultural emissions per animal and per acre of crop production by 2030.
 	In the oil and gas sector, Saskatchewan committed to work with industry to prepare a report and recommendations for the improvement of the industry's efficiency and competitiveness, and to reduce emissions from flaring and venting, along with fugitive emissions, which together make up a very significant share of the province's total GHG emissions.
7:22. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- The Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007-- Sustainable Non-Renewable Resource Sector1
 	As the second-largest oil-producing province in Canada and the third-largest producer of coal and natural gas, Saskatchewan's goal is to continue to reap the economic benefits from these activities, while at the same time recognizing that they account for producing over half of the province's GHG emissions. The Plan saw technology as the principal means by which these emissions could be reduced, principally carbon capture and sequestration, clean coal, polygeneration technology and the reduction of flaring and venting and fugitive emissions from the oil and gas sector. The second tactic was the development of new energy resources, such as oil sands, oil shale and natural gas from coal (although one would speculate that any increase in production in these areas would also increase GHG emissions).
 	Saskatchewan is either the largest or second-largest producer of uranium.2 The Plan called for assessment of the development potential of a nuclear reactor to supply SaskPower with electricity for domestic and export markets.
7:23. Post-Kyoto Policy Development-- The Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007-- Adaptation1
 	In preparation for warmer, drier weather and potentially more severe climate events including drought, heat waves, storm flooding and tornadoes, the Plan's focus was on working with other agencies to develop adaptation strategies. Much of the focus was on working with the Prairie Adaptation Research Collaborative, the Saskatchewan Research Council and the Saskatchewan Forestry Centre to develop a provincial adaptation strategy. The Plan also called for the integration of climate change impacts and adaptation and to risk assessments of business and strategic plans of all Crown corporations and departments. Expanded watershed planning to protect vulnerable water resources was looked upon as a means to allow for specific adaptation measures to be developed at a very local level.

7:24. The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases Act1
 	On May 11, 2009, the Government of Saskatchewan introduced Bill 95, The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases Act ("MRGGA"). It was touted as establishing "a made-in-Saskatchewan plan for reducing GHG emissions to meet provincial targets and promote investments in low-carbon technologies".2 It filled the void left when Saskatchewan abandoned Saskatchewan Energy and Climate Change Plan, 2007 introduced by the previous government.
 	This legislation was reintroduced with minor amendments and assented to in 2010. It was finally proclaimed in 2018 with significant amendments as part of the province's climate strategy, Prairie Resilience. The MRGGA provides the overarching regulatory framework to implement the current government's climate change strategy, Prairie Resilience.
 	The MRGGA empowers the province to set GHG emission reduction targets and to monitor and enforce compliance with them.3 The MRGGA then targets "regulated emitters" by requiring them to establish baseline emission levels for each of their facilities and then to reduce those emissions over time.4 Reduction requirements are specified in a series of regulations emission standards passed pursuant to the MRGGA.
7:25. Regulated Emitters (General)
 	The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases (Standards and Compliance) Regulations1 defines a regulated emitter as an owner or operator of a facility that emitted at least 25,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent in 2017. Emitters with over 10,000 tonnes but less than 25,000 tonnes can apply to be a regulated emitter.2 Regulated emitters are required to be registered3 and determine their baseline emission level and intensity for each product they produce with the assistance of a "qualified person".4 They are then required to reduce their emissions based on the rate of total allowable annual emissions as outlined in the Standard established for that sector.5 The Minister is empowered to set up offset programs to assist emitters in meeting their annual emission targets.6 Finally, regulated emitters are required to regularly file emissions returns to verify compliance with the MRGGA.7
 	Prairie Resilience explains that more than 40 industrial facilities will be subject to emission reductions as they qualify as regulated emitters under The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases (Standards and Compliance) Regulations.8 An overall 10% reduction or 5.3 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent GHGs will be achieved by 2030 through output-based performance standards that will be increased over time.9 The overall reduction will be achieved through 5% intensity reductions in the potash, coal and uranium mining, iron, steel and pulps mills, and fertilizer and ethanol production.10
7:26. Electricity Producers
 	Large electricity producers, however, are subject to their own regulations under The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases (General and Electricity Producer) Regulations.1 The "regulated emitter" under these regulations includes any person who produces in excess of 1.5 million tonnes of GHGs while generating electricity from gas or coal-fired generation and independent power producers (IPPs).2 IPPs are defined as those who generate over 10,000 tonnes per year of carbon dioxide equivalent GHGs from sources other than biomass and flaring or venting gas. Like other regulated emitters, large electricity producers and IPPs are required to submit emission returns3 and are subject to the administrative penalties specified in the MRRGA if they fail to comply with these regulations.4 These regulations impose a cumulative emissions cap of 64.5 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent by 2029.5 This cap, combined with the promise to shift 50% of electricity generation to renewable power, are expected to result in a 40% annual reduction below 2005 emission levels.6
7:27. The Saskatchewan Technology Fund
 	The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases Act has created the Saskatchewan Technology Fund "to support initiatives in Saskatchewan that lead to mitigation, sequestration or capture of greenhouse gases".1 Although the fund is administered by the Minister, it may have an appointed advisory committee to assist the Minister in determining how the fund should be used and administered.2 Revenue in the fund will be generated from carbon compliance payments and any administrative penalties that may be assessed.3
7:28. Administrative, Inspections and Enforcement
 	Part VIII of the The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases Act establishes the procedures by which the Minister can require the production of information, undertake inspections and pursue investigations and maintain copies of records.
7:29. Administrative, Inspections and Enforcement-- Offences and Administrative Penalties
 	As per s. 75(2) of The Management and Reduction of Greenhouse Gases Act, every person who contravenes a provision of the Act, the regulations or the codes is guilty of an offence and is liable on summary conviction to a fine not exceeding $1 million. A person or entity will be in violation of the Act if a false statement or false information is provided, if one omits to state a fact that is required or that is necessary to make a statement not misleading, if one fails to comply with an order of the Minister, or fails to comply with any provision of the Act, the regulations or the codes.1 Any director, office or agent of a corporation who directed, authorized, assented to or acquiesced in or participated in an act or omission of the corporation that would constitute an offence by the corporation is also guilty of an offence and liable to prosecution.2 A court will be able to impose additional penalties by prohibiting the convicted person from doing any act or engaging in any activity that, in the opinion of the court, may result in the continuation of the offence.3 The Minister will be empowered to impose administrative penalties for non-compliance with the Act. Any person assessed an administrative penalty will have the right to appeal the penalty to the Saskatchewan Court of Queen's Bench within 30 days after the date of the Minister's decision to impose the penalty.4
 	One of the main components of Prairie Resilience is the Methane Action Plan, which was released in 2019.5 This initiative aims to reduce methane-based GHG emissions within the province's oil and gas sector with the specific goal of reducing carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) by 4.5 million tonnes annually by 2025.6 At the point of production, companies often have three options for managing methane, which include flaring (burning) the gas, venting (releasing) the gas into the atmosphere, or capturing the gas and making commercial use of it. Of these options, venting is the most emitting. Capture and commercial use is the most environmentally friendly choice, but it is more economically challenging, as North American natural gas prices are low and Saskatchewan lacks province-wide gas-gathering infrastructure. Therefore, Prairie Resilience commits to an increase in commercial uses of methane, such as in heating and electricity production, and directs the focus away from venting and flaring.7
 	The Output-Based Performance Standards initiative also aims to reduce GHG emissions in the oil and gas sector for large industrial emitters. Regulations apply to facilities that emit over 25,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalents annually and mandate a reduction in production emissions intensity. The goal of this initiative is to record a total emissions intensity reduction of 10% by 2030.8
 	In 2022, the province announced that the Output-Based Performance Standards initiative will expand to encompass five additional sectors, including wood product manufacturing, mineral product manufacturing, chemical manufacturing, agricultural and industrial equipment manufacturing, and food and beverage processing. These sectors will save a total of $2.3 billion compared to coverage under the federal industrial pricing system. Additionally, more companies are eligible for coverage because the participation threshold has been reduced to 0 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent.9
 	Following the introduction of these initiatives, methane emissions through venting and flaring totaled 5.2 million tonnes (Mt) of carbon dioxide equivalent in 2020. This is almost a 50% reduction from 2015 levels and is tantamount to removing 1.1 million cars from Saskatchewan roads for one year.10
2. Supplementary Initiatives
7:30. Renewable Fuels
 	In April 2002, the Government of Saskatchewan released its Greenprint for Ethanol Production in Saskatchewan.1 The intent was to create a framework to stimulate the development of an ethanol fuel industry in Saskatchewan. Ethanol was seen as a viable fuel to reduce the dependence of Saskatchewan's residents upon gasoline and as a means of providing new marketing opportunities for Saskatchewan's grain producers and wood product companies. The initiative was also seen as a means by which vehicle GHG emissions could be reduced by as much as 30% where ethanol is blended with gasoline.2
 	At the time, Saskatchewan's ethanol production was approximately 12 million litres per year. Forecasted growth as a result of mandatory ethanol blending of fuels would push required capacity to 130 million litres per year, requiring the construction of additional ethanol production plants. The Greenprint also envisioned a potential export market requiring the resources and providing a marketplace that would produce 400 million litres of fuel per annum. At the time, this was anticipated to create the demand for 1.08 million tonnes of wheat as feedstock, the creation of up to 450 jobs in Saskatchewan and approximately $272.8 million in construction investment.3
 	Apart from ethanol-based fuel, the Greenprint also recognized the opportunity to develop a biodiesel industry through the use of animal fats and vegetable oils from crops, including soy beans, canola, corn and sunflowers.4
 	Building on this initiative, the Saskatchewan government enacted The Ethanol Fuel Act.5 The Act, as implemented through The Ethanol Fuel (General) Regulations,6 required distributors of automotive gasoline fuel to blend ethanol with unleaded automotive gasoline fuel in a manner such that the average volume of the ethanol blend would have had an average composition of at least 1.0% ethanol for the period November 1, 2005 to ending on January 14, 2007.7 This increased to 7.5% ethanol by December 31, 2007 and remains here for all unleaded automotive gasoline fuel.
7:31. Renewable Energy-- Nuclear Energy
 	In 2008, the Government of Saskatchewan established the Uranium Development Partnership for the purpose of examining and reporting upon uranium resources in the Province of Saskatchewan (which was the world's largest producer of uranium at the time) and also to examine exploration, mining, upgrading, power generation, used fuel management, research and development in Saskatchewan. The Uranium Development Partnership submitted its report to government on March 31, 2009.1
 	The Report included several key findings in relation to nuclear power generation.2 Electricity demand growth in Saskatchewan is expected to create a need for between 1,200-1,750 MW by 2020 and between 2,200-3,000 MW by 2030. There may also be export opportunities of between 4,000 and 5,000 MW of electricity to Alberta by 2020. Of this, approximately 3,000 MW of nuclear capacity could be constructed in Saskatchewan. The Report recommended inclusion of nuclear energy as part of Saskatchewan's long-range energy planning mix. As of 2019, Saskatchewan generated 24.1 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity.2.50
 	As a follow-up to the Report, the government established a Future of Uranium in Saskatchewan Public Consultation Process. The Consultation Process was established to carry out public consultations in June of 2009 and focussed on the recommendations contained in the Report. The public consultations revealed that a majority of Saskatchewan residents viewed nuclear energy positively, but nuclear energy proved to be a polarizing issue.3
 	In 2018, Saskatchewan joined National Resources Canada initiative to develop a roadmap for the possible introduction and regulation of small module nuclear reactors in Canada.4 Current Premier Scott Moe has clearly stated that tapping into Saskatchewan's abundant uranium resources is one of the strategies the province is considering to reduce provincial GHG emissions by replacing electricity generation from coal.5
 	Pursuant to his stated intentions, in 2019 Moe announced that the province would look to small modular reactors (SMRs) in the future as part of its goals to lower carbon emissions.6
 	This year, 2022, in April the province announced it had gone ahead with the plan and will be partnering with Ontario, New Brunswick and Alberta to develop SMRs, four of which will be in Saskatchewan.
7:32. Renewable Energy-- Wind Power
 	Wind generation is featured prominently in the Prairie Resilience plan to achieve a 40% reduction in GHG emissions from 2005 levels by increasing total electricity produced by wind to 16%. Saskatchewan currently has nine operational wind power facilities that account for 3% of the province's energy production capacity. Sunbridge Wind Power Facility can produce 11 MW of power and was one of Canada's first, large-scale wind power projects. Other facilities include Cypress (11 MW), Centennial (150 MW), Morse (23 MW), Red Lily (26 MW), Western Lily (20 MW), Riverhurst (10 MW), Blue Hill (175 MW), and Golden South (200 MW).1 A number of these projects are operated independently and sell the power they produce through contracts with SaskPower.2 With the recent announcement that Potentia Renewables Inc. would be constructing the Golden South Wind Energy Facility, Saskatchewan plans to increase wind generation capacity by 200MW. Golden South Wind Facility, the largest facility of its kind in Saskatchewan to date, includes 50 wind turbines is capable of generating enough power for 100,000 homes.3 Golden South is an example of one of the aforementioned projects that are operated independently and sell the power they produce through contracts with SaskPower.
7:33. Renewable Energy-- Solar Energy
 	As part of the province's commitment in Prairie Resilience to increase the amount of electricity generated by renewables to 50% of the province's total energy use, a 60 MW solar energy project has been announced. This project constitutes the first major investment by the Province in solar energy infrastructure.
 	2021 saw the opening of Highfield Solar Facility, the province's first utility-scale solar generation project. The facility includes nearly 30,000 panels, compared to a typical residential project of roughly 20 panels. Highfield is capable of generating enough power for about 2,500 homes.1
7:34. Renewable Energy-- Net Metering Program
 	Through the Saskatchewan Research Council, wind power, low-impact hydro, biomass, heat reclaim, flare gas and photovoltaic electricity generation projects with generating capacities of 100 kW or less and that comply with the area utility's net metering policies are eligible for a one-time payment of up to 20% of the first $100,000 in start-up costs.
7:35. Energy Efficiency
 	Saskatchewan does not have any legislation specific to energy efficiency. Instead, the province has adopted policies directed toward energy efficiency.
 	In addition to the Building Code requirements discussed in §7:11, the province has adopted various programs to assist commercial enterprises with improving energy efficiency. Currently, the province offers rebates of up to 50% to replace commercial refrigeration products and to study and implement more efficient ice rinks. The Parking Lot Controller Program provides $50 per controller installed in parking lots to govern the flow of energy to cars. Finally, the Compressed Air Program provides financial incentives to audit and upgrade compressed air systems.
7:36. Energy Efficiency-- Energy Efficiency--Programs for Individuals and Home Owners
 	The Government of Saskatchewan had implemented several smaller-scale programs to promote energy efficiency and thereby aid in the reduction of the province's GHG emissions. Most of these programs, however, have expired without being renewed.
7:37. Transportation
 	Prairie Resilience does not contain explicit emission reduction targets for transportation even though the sector is responsible for 14% of the province's emissions according to data from 2015. Rather the plan commits to the following:1
  • Monitor the highway system for vulnerabilities that could impact human health, property and transportation efficiency to help prioritize preventative maintenance and redesign.
  • Create a freight strategy to improve delivery times, reduce fuel use, and increase efficiency.
  • Increase the use of idle time limiters in government trucks to reduce fuel usage.
  • Evaluate government fleet vehicles for lower-carbon technology opportunities.
  • Continue to support industry in expanding the size and usage of the short haul (short line) rail systems.
  • Expand the Trucking Partnership Program to increase fuel efficiency in freight hauling.
  • Facilitate traffic data specific to Saskatchewan to mitigate emissions from congestion and idling.
7:38. Transportation-- Anti-Idling
 	The transportation programs introduced by the Government of Saskatchewan are small-scale, largely voluntary and local in focus. The Idle Free Zone program is designed to encourage drivers visiting schools, health facilities, municipal offices and community and recreation centres and government offices to turn off their vehicle engines when not driving. The program provides free outdoor signage to schools, health facilities, community and recreation centres and municipal offices.
 	In addition, the Saskatchewan Ministries of Environment and Government Services have adopted an anti-idling initiative for all government vehicles. The program encourages minimizing idling time and the adoption of driving practices that maximize fuel efficiency.1
3. Carbon Sequestration
7:39. Introduction
 	Sequestration involves the permanent, verifiable capture of carbon dioxide. With a growing population and economy driven largely by the GHG-producing energy and extractive resource industries and little appetite to implement programs that might impede such growth, Saskatchewan has placed considerable reliance upon carbon sequestration as a means of achieving its GHG reduction targets. On one end of the technology spectrum is agricultural biotic sequestration, capturing CO2 in plants and soil. On the opposite end of that spectrum is Saskatchewan's program for the development of large-scale carbon capture and storage projects in the energy sector.
7:40. Biotic Carbon Sequestration
 	Opportunities in Saskatchewan for biotic carbon sequestration have been identified in the agricultural and forestry sectors.
 	Prairie Adaptation Research Collaborative ("PARC") is a partnership of the governments of Canada, Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba mandated to pursue climate change impacts and adaptation research in the Prairie Provinces. In its research,1 PARC identifies Saskatchewan as having a large carbon sink potential in its agricultural land base. Most of this potential is on cropland. Varying levels of sequestration could be achieved through initiatives in the reduction of tillage and summer-fallow, introduction of forages in the crop rotation cycle and better use of manure and compost. Agricultural sequestration carries uncertainties as to the duration and persistence of sequestration. There is also the question of adoption by landowners and the economic incentives required to secure widespread adoption.
 	Under a 50-year agreement, Saskatchewan Environment and SaskPower have created the first forest carbon sequestration project to be formally reviewed and approved in Canada under the Greenhouse Gas Emission Reduction Trading ("GERT") Pilot. It was determined that the project will result in real, measurable, verifiable and surplus net sequestration. The amount of CO2e is calculated as the difference between emissions under the reference (without project) case and the with-project case. The expenditure of some $6 million for the establishment of white spruce plantations by planting five million seedlings on 3300 ha of land and the creation of 206,000 ha of Forest Carbon Reserves is expected to result in a net sequestration of 1.6 Mt. Reviewers considered a number of issues including leakage, the permanence of the sequestration and risk of losses, measuring the benefit, projecting impacts, sampling and measurement, and accounting methods. GERT specified reporting and other conditions to be fulfilled in order to permit registration of the project.2 Nonetheless, rates of zero-tillage have been increasing in Saskatchewan.3
7:41. Carbon Capture and Storage
 	Saskatchewan, primarily through SaskPower, is investing heavily in the development of new industrial carbon dioxide capture and geological sequestration technology. The plan calls for the capture of CO2 from coal-fired electric generating stations and transportation via pipeline to nearby oilfields and its injection into oil-bearing formations to enhance oil recovery. The captured emissions thereby become a commodity that assists in the recovery of additional petroleum. (Of course, the recovered petroleum will eventually be turned into GHG-generating fuel.)
 	In 2018, Whitecap Resources Inc. acquired the largest enhanced oil recovery project in Canada, near Weyburn, Saskatchewan.1 Since the year 2000, 36 Mt have been sequestered at the Weyburn Unit.1.50 When complete, 40 million tonnes of CO2 will have been captured and stored.2
 	In 2006, the Government of Saskatchewan announced an initiative to develop a $1.5 billion, 300 MW near-zero emissions pulverized coal electricity generating plant that was expected to capture about 8,000 tonnes of CO2 per day. It was intended to allow SaskPower to meet emerging regulatory requirements and add generating capacity to utilize Saskatchewan's 300-year supply of low-cost lignite coal.3 Cost escalation that put the project cost at some $3.8 billion resulted in the cancellation of the proposed development in 2007.
 	In 2014, SaskPower completed the Boundary Dam Integrated Carbon Capture and Storage Demonstration Project. The Boundary Dam Project was the world's first commercially viable large-scale carbon capture and storage project at a coal-fired power station. The Boundary Dam reduces approximately 800,000 tonnes of CO2 emissions through carbon capture. The Captured CO2 is transported by pipeline and sold for use in enhanced oil recovery.4 Before further investment in carbon capture, however, an evaluation of the Boundary Dam project with be completes because its costs have far exceeded expectations.
 	The Boundary Dam Project creates opportunities for the sale of other captured by-products. SaskPower currently captures sulphur dioxide and converts it to sulphuric acid for its own use, but intends to begin selling it for industrial use. SaskPower also captures fly-ash, a coal combustion by-product, and sells it for use in ready-mix concrete and other purposes.5
4. Agriculture; Natural Systems; Community Preparedness; Carbon Pricing
7:42. Agriculture
 	Prairie Resilience acknowledges the complicated role agricultural production and agricultural lands play in climate change as both a source and sink of GHGs.1 Consequently, the province has committed to maintaining the total permanent cover on 19.93 million hectares, the level of permanent cover that existed in 2016, and to maintaining the annual amount of soil organic matter sequestered cultivated at 5.60 million tonnes through the promotion of practices such as zero tillage. Finally, the province intends to ensure that 25% of Saskatchewan's cropland has a nutrient stewardship plan by 2025.2
7:43. Natural Systems
 	Prairie Resilience highlights the importance of natural systems in helping the province mitigate and adapt to the effects of climate change. The plan, however, contains few details. Rather, the province promises to maintain or improve existing practices including:1
  • Ensure natural and commercially forested lands are managed in a manner that enhances the removal and storage of carbon from the atmosphere while allowing for sustainable harvesting, respecting normal forest cycles and fire preparedness.
  • Maintain or restore landscape integrity to optimize ecological goods and services, enhance resilience to extreme weather events and manage the risk to biodiversity.
  • Increase our understanding of future climate trends and adaptation options to address impacts that extreme weather events such as flooding and droughts could have on future water supplies.
  • Advance options to improve Saskatchewan's ability to measure stream flows and provide flow forecasts to aid in adapting to the potential effects of climate change on watersheds and infrastructure operations.
  • Continue to implement Saskatchewan's agricultural water management framework in the province to help assure continued productivity, enhance wetland habitat conservation and improve runoff management in times of both drought and flood.
  • Advance options to focus on permanent flood damage reduction and flood mitigation projects and thereby increase resiliency of flood prone areas.
  • Continue to improve the condition and resilience of provincially owned dams and water infrastructure.
 	The only new commitments include creating a carbon offset system for soils and forests and to work with other jurisdictions to establish a coordinated network of parks and conservations areas. There are no timelines, however, associated with these commitments.
7:44. Community Preparedness
 	Prairie Resilience recognizes that communities need the capacity to respond to the effects of a changing climate. As a consequence, some attention is given to helping build resiliency with First Nations, Métis, and municipal communities. As the list of provincial commitments in this area reveals, the emphasis is on examining threats and encouraging these communities to respond rather than taking firm steps to build resiliency:1
  • Examine current floodplain mapping to identify local communities at greatest risk of flooding, fire, and extreme weather events.
  • Encourage family preparedness plans, by making emergency preparedness guides and suggested emergency kit content lists available through Saskatchewan and local government websites.
  • Maintain and enhance partnerships with First Nations and Métis communities to address and adapt to a changing climate through actions that are guided by traditional ecological knowledge.
  • Encourage communities to develop appropriate plans and preparedness to respond and recover from extreme weather events.
  • Encourage municipalities to consider disaster mitigation projects a priority when applying for infrastructure funding.
  • Promote the upgrading of municipal waste and sewage management services to reduce, capture and use GHG emissions and biogas that would otherwise be released into the air, by making these projects a priority category for support under joint federal/provincial funding programs.
7:45. Carbon Pricing
 	Prairie Resilience makes it clear that the made-in-Saskatchewan response to climate change will not include a carbon tax. It describes the federal carbon tax as ineffective and penalizing Saskatchewan over other regions because of Saskatchewan's reliance on agriculture and the oil and gas sectors.1 The promotion of a carbon tax is cited as the primary reason that Saskatchewan has not signed on to the Vancouver Declaration on Clean Growth and Climate Change. It is not surprising, therefore, that following the release of Prairie Resilience, Saskatchewan residents became subject to a federal carbon tax under the Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act.2
B. The Oil and Gas Emissions Management Regulations
7:46. Generally
 	The Oil and Gas Emissions Management Regulations1 supplement the requirements of the MRGGA, but are actually passed pursuant to The Oil and Gas Conservation Act.2 They specifically address operations that flare or vent gas with potential emissions that are greater than 50,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent per year.3 It also regulates emissions from oil facilities.4 Each business subject to these regulations is required to have an emissions reduction plan that is approved by the Minister.5 Failure to comply with that plan is an offence and will result in an administrative penalty that will reach $50 per tonne of excess emissions by 2030.6 The Minister has discretion to defer the payment of any administrative penalty of the business assessed with the penalty is participating in a "qualified conservation project".7
 	The Oil and Gas Emissions Management Regulations are designed to assist Saskatchewan in meeting the target in the 2017 Pan-Canadian Framework of reducing emissions from the oil and gas sector by 40-45% by 2025. Success in meeting this target, however, is conditional on the Saskatchewan government creating a market demand for methane. In addition, a 10% reduction is being set for refining and upgrading and 15% reduction for upstream combustion in this sector.8
VI. Future Trends in Provincial Law and Policy; Regional and International Linkages
7:47. Future Trends in Provincial Law and Policy
 	The future of climate change law and policy in Saskatchewan will involve a complicated balancing act, with a popular and political demand for continued natural resource-led economic growth on one hand and external (national and international) GHG reduction compliance pressures on the other. Saskatchewan's new-found position as a "have" instead of a "have-not" province that is no longer dependent upon federal government transfer payments, the reduction of its reliance on the agricultural sector and its transformation from a sleepy, aging province into a booming, diversified economy and the effects of those changes--economic and attitudinal--should not be underestimated. The understandable reluctance to slow the engines of economic and population growth will make it more difficult for Saskatchewan to achieve its end goals in GHG reduction. Saskatchewan will share many of the same challenges as its fossil fuel producing, resource-rich neighbour, Alberta.
 	It is expected that Saskatchewan will stay true to its legislative and policy agenda of relying on technological innovation to lead emission reductions in the province rather than reducing demand through carbon pricing. This approach has been criticized as falling "well short of what is required for Saskatchewan to do its fair share in reducing greenhouse gas emissions and helping Canada meet its commitments under the Paris Climate Accord".1
7:48. Regional and International Linkages
 	Saskatchewan's opposition to carbon pricing drives its linkages with other jurisdictions. Saskatchewan is the only province not to sign the 2016 Pan-Canadian Framework on Clean Growth and Climate Change, known as the Vancouver Declaration. Saskatchewan has also led litigation efforts to oppose the imposition of a carbon tax in the provinces that fail to price carbon themselves. In 2018, the province asked the Saskatchewan Court of Appeal to rule on the constitutionality of the Greenhouse Gas Pollution Pricing Act. When the court ruled that the federal government had the authority to impose the tax, Saskatchewan appealed.1 A future Supreme Court of Canada decision on this matter will likely shape how Saskatchewan engages regionally and internationally on climate change issues.
VII. Conclusion
7:49. Concluding Remarks
 	Apart from the Arctic and the North, Saskatchewan stands to see some of Canada's most significant climate change impacts. Higher temperatures may mean longer growing seasons, but any positive impacts may be outweighed by increased weather variability and scarcity of water. On the other hand, Saskatchewan's "new economy" is largely resource based and is GHG intensive.
 	In 2007, the Government of Saskatchewan announced a climate change plan that called for 32% per capita GHG emission reductions by 2020. The plan contemplated a broad range of initiatives in electricity conservation, emission reductions in the government sector, carbon sequestration and technology development. The change in government in 2008 brought with it a change in approach to climate change policy, but a stated commitment to achieve the same emission reduction targets. By February of 2009, the stated objective was dropped. It has been replaced by a "Made in Saskatchewan" plan. Prairie Resilience rejects climate pricing and contains what can only be described as unambitious targets to reduce GHGs.
